Why you cannot read your way to the problem

Apex Legends holds a 76.6% positive review score in English and 40.1% in Traditional Chinese. Same game, same patches, same servers. This study asks what causes the gap, rules out three plausible answers, and finds that the reviews themselves cannot supply the fourth.

The question

Steam tags every user review with the language it was written in, and exposes that breakdown through a public API that almost nobody queries. Split any live-service shooter by language and the single headline score falls apart.

The obvious reading is that some player populations are simply harder to please. That reading is wrong, and the study is built around proving it wrong before going any further, because if it were right there would be nothing here for a studio to act on.

Hypothesis 1, rejected

It is not review culture

If Chinese-language reviewers were harsher in general, the gap would appear on every game. Fourteen games were measured across seven languages to test exactly that. The gap is large and consistent on Western live-service shooters, with a median of 18.2 points. On games developed in China it disappears.

Hypothesis 2, rejected

It is not localisation

A bad translation is the first thing people reach for, and it is easy to check. Across every language in the sample, the share of negative reviews mentioning translation, subtitles or language quality never rises above 0.4%. In English it is 0.0%.

Whatever is happening to these players, they are not writing about the words on screen.

Hypothesis 3, rejected

It is not who bothers to write a review

A tempting explanation is composition: perhaps Traditional Chinese reviews come disproportionately from burned-out veterans, and veterans are negative everywhere. The median Traditional Chinese reviewer in this sample had played 373 hours against 80 for the median English reviewer, so the concern is real.

It does not hold. Holding playtime constant, the gap is still there at every level, and it grows the longer people play.

English-speaking players get a honeymoon. Negativity falls to 24.3% once they are twenty hours in. Traditional Chinese players never get one, and by 500 hours they are 31 points more negative than the English players sitting in the same lobbies.

That shape matters more than the headline number. A gap that is widest among new players is an acquisition or first-impression problem. A gap that widens with time played is a retention problem, and it belongs to whoever owns the live service, not to marketing.

What the data will not tell you

The reviews are too short to diagnose

With three explanations ruled out, the obvious next move is to read what the unhappy players actually say. I built keyword sets in five languages for cheating, connection quality, matchmaking, monetisation and performance, then checked them by reading matched reviews.

The check found a real flaw in my own method. One sampled review complained the game “always disconnects mid-fight” using 斷線, a term my list had missed, so the connection theme was being undercounted. I widened it and re-ran. That correction is the only reason the next number is trustworthy.

垃圾伺服器

"Trash servers"

馬鈴薯伺服器

"Potato servers"

我對槍對不贏 垃圾遊戲

"I can't win gunfights, trash game"

Those are real reviews, at or near the median length. The first two are diagnosable. The third is the majority case, and no amount of natural language processing will extract a root cause from it, because there is not one in there.

So the honest conclusion is a negative one, and it is the most useful thing in this study: Steam review text cannot tell a studio why its Chinese-language players are unhappy. The signal that something is wrong is unmistakable and free to collect. The reason is not in the same place, and a team that goes looking for it in review text will spend a quarter and find nothing.

A method note that cost me a day

The sampling window nearly produced a false finding

The collector pulls newest reviews first and stops at a cap. Because languages accumulate reviews at very different rates, each one hit its cap at a different point in history: the English sample covered five months, the Korean sample covered four years.

Compared naively, Simplified Chinese looked 31.9 points below English. Restricted to the window all five languages share, the real figure is 2.2 points. The lifetime gap is genuine but historical: English sentiment has since fallen to meet it.

Every cross-language number on this page is computed inside the shared window for that reason. It is the single easiest way to publish something confidently wrong.

A useful by-product

Review bombs are separable from real decline

A review bomb has a signature: volume spikes while score collapses. Ordinary decline moves the score without moving the volume. Two events fall straight out of the monthly series.

Japanese reviews in July 2024 ran at 3.9 times normal volume and 19% positive against a 43% median. Korean reviews in July and August 2024 ran at 3.0 and 3.4 times volume, at 25% and 28% against a 67% median. Traditional Chinese shows no such spike at any point in two years of data: its low score is chronic, not an event.

That distinction is worth having on a dashboard. One of those needs a community response this week. The other needs a roadmap.

What I would tell a live ops team

  • Split the review score by language on the dashboard. The aggregate hid a 36 point spread and it hid it in both directions: Japanese and Korean players rate Overwatch 2 about 11 points better than the baseline predicts.
  • Treat the language gap as a retention metric, not a quality metric, because it widens with hours played.
  • Do not budget for sentiment mining on CJK review text. Fund an in-client survey instead. The reviews say that something is wrong and will not say what.
  • Flag volume spikes and score drops separately so a review bomb never enters a trend line as a real decline.

Limits of this study

  • Steam reviewers are not the playerbase. They self-select for strong opinions.
  • Language is a proxy for region, not a measurement of it. A Taiwanese player writing in English is counted as English.
  • Console players are absent entirely. This is Steam only, and Steam is a minority platform for this game in some of these territories, which may itself skew who is represented.
  • Keyword topic detection is a blunt instrument built by a non-native speaker of four of the five languages. It was verified by sampling, and one error was found and corrected, but others may remain.
  • Review timestamps record when somebody wrote, not when they played or quit.
  • Nothing here establishes causation. It rules out three explanations and locates the remaining problem in the player lifecycle. It does not name it.

Method

All data comes from Steam's public review API, collected with two Python scripts using only the standard library. collect_baseline.py pulls review summaries for 14 games across 7 languages and validates every app id against Steam so a mistyped id cannot enter the study. collect_reviews.py pulls the full Apex Legends review set per language, keeping the text, the timestamp, the verdict and the playtime recorded at the moment of writing.

Analysis is pandas. The 14 game sample deliberately includes two Chinese-developed games and two older Western live-service games as controls, because without them the review-culture hypothesis cannot be separated from the rest. Raw data, scripts and the full brief, including the hypotheses written before the data was collected, are in the repository.
