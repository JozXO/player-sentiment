Steam review analysis · 218,380 reviews · September 2026

Why you cannot read your way to the problem

Apex Legends holds a 76.6% positive review score in English and 40.1% in Traditional Chinese. Same game, same patches, same build. This study rules out three explanations for the gap, finds that a single review-bombing event was faking a fourth, and ends on what the reviews themselves cannot tell anyone.

The question

Steam tags every user review with the language it was written in and exposes that breakdown through a public API almost nobody queries. Split any live-service shooter by language and the single headline score falls apart.

The obvious reading is that some player populations are harder to please. The study is built around testing that first, because if it were true there would be nothing here to act on.

Hypothesis 1, rejected

It is not review culture

If Chinese-language reviewers were harsher in general, the gap would appear on every game. Fourteen games were measured across seven languages to test exactly that. On Western live-service shooters the gap is large and consistent, with a median of 18.2 points. On games developed in China it disappears.

Hypothesis 2, rejected

It is not localisation

A bad translation is the first thing people reach for and the easiest to check. Across 47,000 negative reviews in five languages, the share mentioning translation, subtitles or language quality never rises above 0.1%. In English it is 0.0%.

Whatever is happening to these players, they are not writing about the words on screen.

The finding that changed the study

One weekend in July 2024 was faking a regional problem

A review bomb has a signature: volume spikes while score collapses. Ordinary decline moves the score and leaves the volume alone. In July 2024 every language spiked at once, but not equally.

That event sits inside the comparison window, so every gap in the study was carrying it. Removing July to September 2024 and recalculating:

The Simplified Chinese gap was 20.8 points and is really 4.1. Four fifths of it was one community protest, not a regional quality problem. Korean inverts entirely.

This is the part with a cost attached. A team that skipped this step would have opened a workstream into Simplified Chinese player experience, and found nothing, because there is barely anything there. Two of the four gaps survive the check. Two do not.

Hypothesis 3, rejected

It is not who bothers to write a review

The remaining explanation is composition: perhaps Traditional Chinese reviews come disproportionately from burned-out veterans, and veterans are negative everywhere. The median Traditional Chinese reviewer here had played 578 hours against 107 for the median English reviewer, so the concern is real.

It does not hold. Holding playtime constant, the gap is there at every level.

English-speaking players get a honeymoon. Negativity falls to 31.2% once they are twenty hours in. Traditional Chinese players never get one. Japanese players start level with English and end 20 points below them.

The shapes matter more than the sizes. Japanese is a clean late-souring curve: at under 20 hours the gap is +1.1 points with a confidence interval that includes zero, and by 500 hours it is +20.0. Nothing is wrong with the Japanese first impression. Something goes wrong later.

Traditional Chinese is different again: the gap is already +9.6 in the first 20 hours, peaks at +25.4 between 100 and 500, then eases to +21.8. It is not a first-impression problem and it is not purely a veteran problem. It is present throughout.

Either way, a gap that grows with hours played is a retention signal, not a quality score, and it belongs to whoever owns the live service.

What the data will not tell you

The reviews are too short to diagnose

With three explanations rejected and one exposed as an artefact, the obvious next move is to read what the unhappy players say. I built keyword sets in five languages for cheating, connection quality, matchmaking, monetisation and performance, then checked them by reading matched reviews.

The check found a flaw in my own method. One sampled review complained the game “always disconnects mid-fight” using 斷線, a term my list had missed, so the connection theme was undercounted. I widened it and re-ran.

垃圾伺服器

"Trash servers"

馬鈴薯伺服器

"Potato servers"

我對槍對不贏 垃圾遊戲

"I can't win gunfights, trash game"

Those are real reviews at or near the median length. The first two are diagnosable. The third is the majority case, and no amount of language processing extracts a root cause from it, because there is not one in there.

So the conclusion is a negative one, and it is the most useful thing here: Steam review text cannot tell a studio why its Chinese-language players are unhappy. The signal that something is wrong is unmistakable and free to collect. The reason is somewhere else, and a team that goes looking for it in review text will spend a quarter and find nothing.

Method note

The same question, three defensible windows, three answers

The collector pulls newest reviews first and stops at a cap. Because languages accumulate reviews at very different rates, each hits its cap at a different point in history, so a naive pull compares five different time periods.

Fixing that is not one decision, it is several, and the Simplified Chinese gap moves every time:

TreatmentSimplified Chinese gap
No window control31.9 pts
Shared window, 5 months2.2 pts
Shared window, 2 years20.8 pts
Shared window, 2 years, review bomb removed4.1 pts

Every one of those is a defensible choice and they disagree by 30 points. The first version of this study published the second row and called it the answer. It was not. The window has to be justified, not just applied, and known events inside it have to be excluded before anything is attributed to a region.

What I would tell a live ops team

  • Split the review score by language on the dashboard. The aggregate hid a 36 point spread, and it hid it in both directions: Japanese and Korean players rate Overwatch 2 about 11 points better than the genre baseline predicts.
  • Exclude review-bombing events before attributing anything to a region. Four fifths of one gap in this study was a single protest month.
  • Treat the language gap as a retention metric, not a quality metric, because it grows with hours played.
  • Do not budget for sentiment mining on CJK review text. Fund an in-client survey instead. The reviews say something is wrong and will not say what.

Limits of this study

  • Steam reviewers are not the playerbase. They self-select for strong opinions.
  • Language is a proxy for region, not a measurement of it. A Taiwanese player writing in English is counted as English.
  • Console players are absent entirely. Steam only, and Steam is a minority platform for this game in some of these territories, which may itself skew who is represented.
  • Keyword topic detection is a blunt instrument built by a non-native speaker of four of the five languages. It was verified by sampling and one error was found and corrected; others may remain.
  • Removing July to September 2024 removes a real event that real players experienced. It is the right call for measuring a persistent regional gap and the wrong call for measuring community sentiment.
  • Nothing here establishes causation. It rejects three explanations, removes a fourth, and locates the remaining problem in the player lifecycle. It does not name it.

Method

All data comes from Steam's public review API, collected with two Python scripts using only the standard library. collect_baseline.py pulls review summaries for 14 games across 7 languages and validates every app id against Steam so a mistyped id cannot enter the study. collect_reviews.py pulls the full Apex Legends review set per language, keeping the text, the timestamp, the verdict and the playtime recorded at the moment of writing. 218,380 reviews were collected, of which 114,739 fall inside the shared window with the review bomb removed.

Analysis is pandas. The 14 game sample deliberately includes two Chinese-developed games and two older Western live-service games as controls, because without them the review-culture hypothesis cannot be separated from the rest. Raw data, scripts and the full brief, including the hypotheses written before the data was collected, are in the repository.
